Rent increase laws by state

Most states do not cap rent increases. Of the 45 jurisdictions checked against their own statutes (states plus the District of Columbia), 4 cap how much rent can rise in a year (California, District of Columbia, Oregon, Washington) and 41 set no statewide cap. What many do regulate is notice: 19 of the 45 require written notice before an increase takes effect on a month-to-month tenancy, from 30 to 90 days. The table links each state's own law.

This is general information, not legal advice. Each row was read on the state's own legislative site on or about October 31, 2026; rules change, cities add their own, and many units are exempt. Read the linked source before acting on a row.

Which states cap rent increases?

4 of the 45 jurisdictions checked: California, District of Columbia, Oregon, Washington. California, Oregon and Washington tie the limit to inflation with a ceiling, so the figure changes every year and a state agency publishes it. California caps covered units at 5% plus local inflation, never above 10%, and for increases starting August 1, 2026 that is 8.2% in San Diego County (see how much a landlord can raise rent and the calculator). Oregon and Washington use 7% plus inflation, also capped at 10%. The District of Columbia caps increases on units in its rent stabilization program at inflation plus 2%. All four exempt newer buildings and other housing, so a unit in a capped state is not always a capped unit. The other 41 set no statewide limit, so the lease and the market decide; some, such as New Jersey and New York, let cities set rules instead.

How much notice does a rent increase need?

It depends on the state and, in several, on how long you have lived there. Among the 45 states checked, 2 require at least 90 days' written notice on a month-to-month tenancy (Oregon, Washington), 7 require 60 days (Colorado, Delaware, District of Columbia, Georgia, Nevada, Rhode Island, Vermont), and 10 require 30 days (Alaska, California, Hawaii, Idaho, Iowa, Maine, New Hampshire, New York, North Dakota, South Dakota). 26 have no statute on notice for an increase at all (Alabama, Arizona, Connecticut, Florida, Illinois, Kansas, Kentucky, Louisiana, Maryland, Massachusetts, Michigan, Minnesota, Missouri, Montana, Nebraska, New Jersey, North Carolina, Ohio, Oklahoma, Pennsylvania, South Carolina, Texas, Utah, Virginia, Wisconsin, Wyoming); there, the lease governs, and the only statutory floor is the notice needed to end a month-to-month tenancy, usually one rental period. Some states add longer periods for longer tenancies or older tenants. A text or a call is not written notice where writing is required.

Can my city pass its own rent control?

In 24 of the 45 states checked, no: state law bars cities and counties from controlling rent (Alabama, Arizona, Colorado, Florida, Idaho, Illinois, Iowa, Kansas, Massachusetts, Michigan, Missouri, Montana, Nebraska, North Carolina, North Dakota, Ohio, Oklahoma, Oregon, South Carolina, South Dakota, Texas, Utah, Washington, Wisconsin). In 4 the state allows local ordinances (California, Minnesota, New Jersey, New York), which is why New Jersey municipalities can adopt rent control with no state cap, and why rent stabilization in New York applies in New York City and the localities that adopt it but not statewide. For 17 states no preemption statute was found on a primary source (Alaska, Connecticut, Delaware, District of Columbia, Georgia, Hawaii, Kentucky, Louisiana, Maine, Maryland, Nevada, New Hampshire, Pennsylvania, Rhode Island, Vermont, Virginia, Wyoming), so the table says "not checked" rather than guessing. Where local control is allowed, the city ordinance is the document to read; the state table above only tells you whether one can exist.

Can rent go up during a fixed-term lease?

Usually not. A fixed-term lease sets the rent for the term, and in most states the landlord cannot change a term of the agreement until it ends unless the lease itself contains a clause allowing a mid-term increase. The notice rules above apply when the term ends and a new term or a month-to-month tenancy begins. Washington's statute says an increase may not take effect before the end of the rental term; Oregon bars any increase in the first year of a tenancy. Read your own lease for an escalation clause before assuming either way.
