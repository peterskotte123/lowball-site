How do you calculate a rent increase percentage?

Subtract the current rent from the new rent to get the increase in dollars. Divide that by the current rent and multiply by 100. For the example above: $2,700.00 minus $2,400.00 is $300.00, and $300.00 ÷ $2,400.00 is 0.125, or 12.5%. To go the other way, from a cap to a rent, multiply the current rent by one plus the cap: $2,400.00 × 1.082 is $2,596.80, the most a covered San Diego County unit at $2,400.00 can reach. Always divide by the old rent, not the new one: $300.00 ÷ $2,700.00 is 11.1%, which understates the increase. In California the cap is measured from the lowest rent charged in the prior 12 months, and increases within 12 months count together (Civ. Code 1947.12).

Is my rent increase legal in California?

It comes down to three checks. First, is the unit covered by Civil Code 1947.12? Many are not: housing with a certificate of occupancy issued within the previous 15 years, many single-family homes and condos whose owner gave the written exemption notice the statute requires, owner-occupied duplexes, deed-restricted affordable housing, and others. The cap page lists them. Second, if it is covered, is the increase within the cap for your area? For increases starting August 1, 2026 through July 31, 2027, the Attorney General's published caps are 8.2% in San Diego County, 8.7% in Los Angeles and Orange, 8.1% in Riverside and San Bernardino, 8.8% in the San Francisco area, and 8.6% elsewhere. An increase over 10% is above every one of them. Third, was the notice in writing and on time (Civil Code 827)? Some cities have stricter rules of their own.

How much notice does a rent increase need?

In California, Civil Code 827 covers week-to-week and month-to-month tenancies. The landlord must give written notice at least 30 days before the increase if it, together with any other increases in the past 12 months, is 10% or less; above 10%, the notice period is 90 days. A notice mailed to an address in California adds five calendar days (CCP 1013(a)). The Attorney General says a call, text or email is not enough. In Alabama no statute sets a notice period for an increase, so the lease governs; a month-to-month tenancy can be ended on 30 days' written notice before a rental date (Ala. Code 35-9A-441(b)). On a fixed-term lease the rent usually stays put until the term ends, since rent is a term of the agreement (Ala. Code 35-9A-161).

What can you do about an increase?

Renters who get an increase usually reply in one of a few ways: accept it, counter with a smaller number, ask for something else in exchange, such as a longer lease, or plan a move. A landlord weighs a counter against the cost of an empty unit: rent lost while it sits, cleaning and repairs before the next tenant, and the time or fees to re-let it. A counter that points to comparable listings nearby asking less is one the landlord can check. Our renewal tool puts numbers on those turnover costs, shows its assumptions, and drafts a reply. For how others word a counter, see how to negotiate rent. In Alabama there is no cap, so the lease and the conversation are what you have (Ala. Code 11-80-8.1). The landlord can say no.
