What is a landlord reference letter?

It is a short letter from a current or former landlord confirming that you rented from them and how the tenancy went. A new landlord uses it to answer three questions an application cannot: did this person pay on time, did they look after the home, and would the last landlord rent to them again. Many applications ask for a landlord's contact details and make the call themselves; a signed letter in hand speeds that up and helps when your landlord is slow to answer the phone. The template above writes both the letter and a short message asking your landlord to sign it. Keep it to facts the landlord can stand behind, because the new landlord may call to confirm them.

What should a landlord reference letter include?

Six things, all of them checkable. The tenant's name and the address rented. The dates of the tenancy. The monthly rent. Whether rent was paid in full and on time. The condition the home was kept in and whether there were complaints. And whether the landlord would rent to the tenant again, which is the line a new landlord reads first. It ends with the landlord's name, signature, and a phone number or email for confirmation. It does not need to be long or warm; half a page is normal. It should not include anything about the tenant's family, health, religion or background, which have no place in a rental decision.

How do you ask your landlord for a reference?

Ask in writing, make it easy, and ask before you give notice if you can. A landlord is more likely to sign a letter that is already drafted than to write one, which is why the template fills in the dates and rent for them to check. Say what it is for and when you need it, and offer to let them send their own version. If you rent from a large property manager, ask the office for a rental verification; many have a standard form and will not sign yours. If your landlord will not give a reference, a ledger or bank records showing twelve months of on-time rent make much of the same case, and you can offer those with your application.

Can a reference help you get a lower rent?

It can help more than people expect. A landlord choosing between applicants is weighing risk as much as price: a tenant who pays late or leaves early costs far more than a small discount. Proof that you pay on time and stay put is something you can offer in place of money, alongside things like a longer lease or a flexible move-in date. So send the reference with your offer, not after it.
