Nobody knows what rent should cost

If rent feels impossible, that is not a you problem. 67% of Gen Z and 53% of millennials say they struggle to afford rent or a mortgage. Half of all tenants spend more than 30% of their income on it; 27% spend more than 70%.

And almost nobody on either side of the deal knows what a place is actually worth. Individual owners, not corporations, own about 70% of US rental properties — smaller properties, about 38% of all rental units — and the standard advice for pricing one is to look at a few nearby listings and adjust. But those listings are asking prices, not signed rents. A listing that has been sitting is a high guess — and it becomes the comparison for the next owner down the street. The error does not correct itself. It spreads.

In our own San Diego data, listings that have sat ask 3.6% more than fresh listings with the same number of bedrooms in the same ZIP. The places that sit are usually the overpriced ones, and they are the ones everyone else is pricing against.

That is the gap Lowball closes. It prices a listing against every comparable listing nearby at once, then tells you how listings like it have actually behaved: how many cut, by how much, and how much longer they sat.

Sources: Redfin renter survey; Americans for Financial Reform; US Census Rental Housing Finance Survey via Congressional Research Service. The 3.6% figure is from our own September 2026 San Diego County snapshot.

How it works

Every number comes with its evidence: nearby comps, days on market, price cuts, posted specials, and the listing site's own rent estimate shown for comparison. You see the reasoning, not just a figure.

1

Triage a whole search page

Search results get a chip on each card: how much a month it asks above similar listings nearby, or at market, or under, with an opening number. Triage the whole page at a glance.

2

See how it compares

The panel opens with what happens next: days listed, the share of listings like it that have already cut, and how much longer it is likely to sit. Then the offer, the difference over a year if it is accepted, and why. Big buildings get per-unit numbers and the posted specials called out.

3

Walk away, or ask

Most of the time the answer is simple. If a place asks well over what similar listings nearby get and you do not love it, move on and spend the afternoon somewhere better. If you do want it, one click drafts a polite, evidence-backed note with one number and the comps behind it.

If they will not cut the rent

Some buildings never lower rent. They offer a month free instead. That is a real discount this year — but your lease usually still says the higher number, and next year's increase is typically calculated from that, not from what you actually paid.

You pay $2,610

A $2,847 unit with one month free on a 12-month lease. That is your effective rent.

Your lease says $2,847

A concession usually does not change the number on the agreement, and that number is what carries forward.

Year two: $2,961

A 4% increase, applied to $2,847. Against the $2,610 you were actually paying, that is a 13.4% jump.

The day to deal with this is the day you sign, when you have nothing at stake. At renewal you have moving costs, a deposit and time off work all arguing against you. Many renters in this position ask for both: the free month, and either a lower base rent or a cap on the renewal increase, written into the lease.

Lowball reads posted specials and shows the effective rent behind them, so you can compare the real number against the lease number before you sign.

What we collect: listings, not people

To score a listing, the extension sends that listing's facts to our server: price, beds, baths, size, location, days listed, price history. Nothing about you. If you opt in, the listings you view also help improve estimates for everyone. No names, emails, or searches, ever. Turn it off any time from the panel.
