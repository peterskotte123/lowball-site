Month-to-month lease: how it works

A month-to-month lease is a rental agreement that renews every month until the landlord or the tenant gives notice. It trades certainty for flexibility: you can leave on short notice, and the rent can change on short notice too. Of the 45 states we checked, 19 require written notice before a rent increase, from 30 to 90 days.

What is a month-to-month lease?

It is a tenancy with no end date. The agreement runs for one month at a time and renews automatically when rent is paid, until one side gives written notice to end it. It can start that way, with a month-to-month agreement signed at move-in, or it can follow a fixed lease: in many states, when a one-year lease ends and the tenant stays and the landlord keeps accepting rent, the tenancy continues month to month on the same terms unless the lease says something else. Your lease's renewal or holdover section says which applies to you. Everything else in the agreement, such as the deposit, the rules and who pays utilities, normally carries on unchanged. Only the length of the commitment differs.

Can a landlord raise rent on a month-to-month lease?

Yes, and more easily than on a fixed lease, because there is no term holding the rent in place. What limits it is notice, and in a few places a cap. Among the 45 states we checked against their own statutes, 19 require written notice before an increase takes effect, and 9 of those require 60 days or more (Colorado, Delaware, District of Columbia, Georgia, Nevada, Oregon, Rhode Island, Vermont, Washington). The other 26 set no notice period for an increase at all, so the agreement governs, and a landlord who wants more rent can usually end the tenancy on the notice the state requires and offer a new one. 4 limit the size of an increase on covered units (California, District of Columbia, Oregon, Washington). How much notice do you need to end a month-to-month lease?

It depends on the state and on the agreement, and the two sides do not always owe the same notice. Thirty days in writing is the most common figure, and some states ask for more from the landlord than from the tenant or lengthen it for long tenancies. Among the states in our table that set no notice for a rent increase, the statutes we read put the notice to end a month-to-month tenancy at 10 to 60 days (9 states). Check three things before you give notice: the number of days your agreement requires, whether notice must line up with the end of a rental period, and how it must be delivered. Notice given a day late can cost a further month's rent. This is general information, not legal advice.

Is month-to-month more expensive than a fixed lease?

Often, yes. A landlord takes on more risk when a tenant can leave on a month's notice, since an empty unit earns nothing, and many charge a higher rent or a monthly fee for the flexibility. Large apartment buildings often print it on the renewal offer: one price for twelve months, a higher one for month to month. The size of the difference varies by building and by season, so read the offer; there is no standard figure. The other cost is uncertainty, because the rent can be raised again after one notice period. Month to month earns its price when you expect to move within a few months, are waiting on a job or a home purchase, or want to leave without paying to break a lease.

Can you negotiate a month-to-month rent?

Yes, and the landlord's own arithmetic is your best argument. An empty unit costs a landlord weeks of rent plus cleaning and re-letting, so a tenant who stays is worth something. If the month-to-month price is higher than the lease price, ask what a shorter commitment would cost: three or six months often lands near the twelve-month rent. If you have been handed an increase, compare the new rent with what similar units nearby are asking before you answer.
