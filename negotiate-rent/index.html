Can you negotiate rent on an apartment?

Yes, you can usually negotiate rent, especially on a listing that has been up a month or more: 30% of rental listings 31 to 60 days old have already cut their price, by a median 5.0%. Measured on 1,566 listings in that band, mostly in San Diego County, as of 2026-10-30.

Is rent negotiable at all?

Usually, yes. An asking rent is the landlord's opening number, not a fixed price, and many private landlords have room to move because an empty unit earns nothing. Some will not move at all, especially in a building where pricing software sets the rent each day. You lose very little by asking politely with a reason the landlord can check: the worst answer is no, and you can still rent the place at the asking price. What changes the odds is evidence. "Can you do better?" is easy to refuse. "Similar homes nearby ask less, and this one has been listed for seven weeks" is a business case.

When does negotiating rent work?

It works best on listings that have sat. After a month without a tenant, a price cut is common. Why can "days on Zillow" undercount?

The counter on a Zillow listing restarts when the landlord relists the unit or refreshes the listing in place, so a home that has been available for two months can show two weeks. In a study of 6,684 live San Diego rentals on 2026-10-22, 15.5% carried a relist or a reset clock, and for those the page showed a median of 16 days against a median of 50 days the listing had actually been available. The listing's own price history usually shows the first date it was listed, and that is the date worth naming in an offer, because the landlord can check it.

Can you negotiate rent at an apartment complex?

Yes, but ask for the right thing. Large buildings tend to protect the base rent, which sets the value of the building and the starting point for every renewal, and move concessions instead: a few weeks or a month free, waived fees, or free parking for the first year. Ask what specials are running on units like yours, and whether a longer lease or a later move-in date gets a bigger one. Compare offers by the net effective rent, the total you pay over the lease divided by its months, so "6 weeks free" and "$150 off" can be put side by side. See how net effective rent works. A renewal increase is often figured from the base rent, not the effective one, so a lower base is worth more than the same saving as free weeks.

How much lower should you offer?

Base the opener on things the landlord can verify. Lowball sets one from four inputs: what comparable listings nearby ask, the listing's own price history, what another month empty costs the landlord, and a cap so the number is never insulting. For example, take a made-up 2-bed apartment asking $3,200, listed 45 days, already cut once from $3,350. Similar 2-beds nearby ask a median of $3,050. Another month empty costs the landlord $3,200, about $267 a month over a 12-month lease. An opener near $2,975, with $3,050 as a fair place to settle, is grounded in all three. A fresh listing priced at or under its neighbors leaves little room, and the honest offer there is close to asking.

What can you offer besides money?

Landlords pay for vacancy, turnover, collections and repairs, so anything that lowers those costs is worth something. These are the terms the Lowball extension can add to an offer letter, and you should only offer what is true for you:

  • A longer lease, 18 or 24 months, which pushes the next turnover out.
  • A flexible move-in date, or a specific date that fills the gap sooner.
  • Autopay rent and renters insurance from day one.
  • Taking the unit as-is for cosmetic items only. Safety and habitability stay the landlord's job.
  • A landlord reference and a credit check ready to run.
  • No parking needed, when the unit comes with a space the landlord can rent separately.

New tenant or renewal?

As a new tenant you have the most leverage on a listing that has sat. The landlord is paying for every empty day, and you are a qualified applicant ready to sign. Make your offer before you apply, back it with comparable listings and the listing's age, and add one or two of the terms above.

At renewal the math is different. You are not choosing between listings, the landlord is weighing your increase against the cost of you leaving: an empty month or more, cleaning and repairs, and the cost of finding someone new. That cost is often larger than the increase. Compare your renewal to what the unit would rent for today, and check how much a landlord can raise rent where you live.

How do you negotiate rent, step by step?

  1. Check the listing's real age in its price history, not only the day counter.
  2. Look up what similar homes nearby are asking.
  3. Pick an opening number and the most you would pay.
  4. Name what you offer besides money.
  5. Send it in writing, short and polite, before you apply.
  6. Be ready for a counter, and know which terms you can trade.

Do not bluff or claim another offer you do not have. The landlord can say no, and a no costs you nothing:

Hi [Landlord name],

I'm interested in [address or unit] and can move in by [date]. I see it was first listed on [date]. Similar [beds]-bedroom rentals nearby are listed at [range]. Would you consider [offer] a month? I can sign a [months]-month lease and set up autopay, and my previous landlord, [name], can give a reference.

Thank you,
[Your name]
[Phone]
