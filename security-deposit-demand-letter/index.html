What is a security deposit demand letter?

It is a short, dated letter asking a former landlord to return a security deposit that has not come back, or to explain in writing what was kept. It states the address, the day you moved out, the amount you paid, the amount you are owed, the law that sets the deadline, and a date by which you expect payment. It is the normal first step before small claims court, and judges expect to see that you asked in writing first. Most deposit disputes end here, because a landlord who has missed a statutory deadline usually prefers paying to appearing in court. Keep the tone factual. How long does a landlord have to return a security deposit?

It depends on the state. Of the 50 states (counting DC) whose rule we read on a legislature, court or state agency page, 49 set a deadline, from 14 to 60 days, and 38 of those give the landlord 30 days or fewer. Each of them requires the landlord to state in writing what was deducted. The clock usually starts when the tenancy ends and you hand back the keys, and in some states it starts only once you have given a forwarding address in writing. Several states use a shorter deadline when nothing is deducted or allow the lease to change it. The table below has each state's rule with a link to the page we read it on. What can a landlord deduct from a deposit?

In general, unpaid rent and the cost of repairing damage beyond normal wear and tear, plus cleaning to return the home to the condition it was in when you moved in, where the lease and the state allow it. Normal wear and tear is the decline that comes from living somewhere: faded paint, worn carpet in walkways, small nail holes. Damage is what goes beyond that, such as holes in walls, burns, stains or broken fixtures. A landlord generally cannot charge you for the last tenant's damage or for upgrades. Your best evidence is a move-in checklist, dated photos from move-in and move-out, and receipts for any cleaning. Each state defines the allowed deductions in its own statute, linked in the table.

What if the landlord ignores the letter?

The next step is usually small claims court, which is built for disputes of this size and does not require a lawyer. You file in the county where the rental is, pay a filing fee, and bring your lease, proof of the deposit, your move-out photos, the demand letter and proof you sent it. Many states let a tenant recover more than the deposit when a landlord keeps it in bad faith or misses the deadline; the amount and the conditions differ, so read your state's statute before you name a figure. Send the letter by a method that proves delivery, such as certified mail with a return receipt, and keep a copy. Your state court's self-help site explains filing.

Moving somewhere new?

A deposit dispute is easier to avoid than to win, so start the next tenancy with evidence: photograph every room on the day you move in, note existing damage in writing, and send it to the landlord so there is a dated record.
