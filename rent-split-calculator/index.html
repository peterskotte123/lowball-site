Rent split calculator

To split rent fairly, take off a flat amount for anything only one room has, then divide the rest: equally, by room size, or half each way. On $3,000 with rooms of 180, 140 and 120 square feet and a $75 private bathroom, the half-and-half split is $1,161, $953 and $886.

$3,000 less $75 of extras leaves $2,925 to divide: half of it equally, half by each room's share of 440 sq ft. Extras are then added to the room that has them.

"Extra" is a flat amount a room pays on top for something only it has, such as a private bathroom or the parking space. Do not know the sizes? Measure length times width, or put 1 for every room and use extras only. The calculator runs in your browser and sends nothing anywhere.

How do you split rent fairly between roommates?

Agree on the method before anyone picks a room. There are three common ones. An equal split is simplest and works when the rooms are close in size. A split by room size charges each person for their share of the bedroom square footage, which suits a home where one room is much larger. The middle path divides half the rent equally, because everyone uses the kitchen and living room the same, and half by room size. On $3,000 with bedrooms of 180, 140 and 120 square feet and no extras, a pure size split is $1,227, $955 and $818; an equal split is $1,000 each. The calculator above defaults to the middle path. Whichever you choose, write the amounts down and have everyone confirm them.

How do you split rent by room size?

Measure each bedroom, length times width, and add the sizes together. Each room's share of the rent is its size divided by the total. With rooms of 180, 140 and 120 square feet the total is 440, so the largest room is 180 ÷ 440, or 41% of the rent. Count only private space: bedrooms, and a closet or balcony that opens off one. Leave shared rooms out, since everyone pays for those through the split itself. A pure size split can feel steep for the big room, because it treats the whole rent as if it bought bedrooms, when a good part of it buys the kitchen, the living room and the location. That is the case for dividing only half the rent by size.

How much extra is a private bathroom or parking worth?

There is no standard figure, so treat it as a flat amount the household agrees on, paid on top by the room that has it. The calculator takes that amount off the rent first, divides what is left, then adds it back to that room. In the example, a $75 private bathroom makes the equal split $1,050, $975 and $975 in place of $1,000 each. One way to find the number is to ask what each person would pay to have the feature and settle near the middle of the answers. The same works for the only parking space, a walk-in closet, or the room with the better light. A room with a drawback, such as one beside the front door or with no closet, can carry a negative amount in the conversation: lower its rent and spread the difference.

Should a couple sharing a room pay more?

Usually yes, and the question is how much. Splitting by room treats the couple as one tenant; splitting by person treats them as two. Take a $2,400 two-bedroom with a couple in one room and a single person in the other. By room, the couple pays $1,200. By person, with three people, the couple pays $1,600. Many households land between the two, here $1,400 for the couple and $1,000 for the single roommate, on the reasoning that two people use more of the shared space and utilities than one but still occupy one bedroom. Utilities are easier: split those by person. To model a couple in the calculator, enter the amount they agree to pay on top as that room's extra.

Does the landlord care how roommates split the rent?

Generally no. When all the roommates sign one lease, the landlord is owed the full rent each month and the lease usually makes every tenant responsible for all of it, not only for a share. How you divide it among yourselves is a private arrangement. That is why it is worth putting the split in writing, in a short roommate agreement or even a message everyone replies to: the amounts, who pays the landlord, what happens if someone moves out early, and how the deposit comes back. Read your own lease for how it handles a roommate leaving. This is general information, not legal advice.

Is the total rent fair in the first place?

A fair split of an overpriced apartment still costs everyone too much. Before you sign, check the total against what similar homes nearby are asking and how long this one has been listed, because a place that has sat for weeks often has room to come down, and a lower rent lowers every share.
